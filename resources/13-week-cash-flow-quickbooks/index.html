01What You Need

What You Need, and Connecting Claude to QuickBooks

Three things have to be true: a Claude plan with file creation switched on, because that's how the workbook comes back to you, access to the client's QuickBooks Online company, and about twenty minutes the first time.

Claude plans. Check current plans and prices at claude.com/pricing. For a firm, use Team: client data sits under business terms and isn't used to train Claude's models, and you get central billing and admin controls. If you use an individual plan (Pro or Max), open Settings, then Privacy, and turn off the setting that lets Anthropic use your chats to improve Claude, before you discuss any client figures.

The QuickBooks connector. Intuit has an official QuickBooks connector in Claude. You add it from Connectors in Claude and sign in with your Intuit login. Since July 2026 it can also create, send and delete invoices, estimates and payment links. For this forecast, use it for reading only. Never ask it to create, send or change anything in the client's books.

What the connector does here, and what the exports do. Public write-ups disagree on how much transaction-level detail the connector returns. So this guide uses the connector for quick checks: the bank balances, the profit and loss, and confirming the company name. The forecast itself is built from QuickBooks report exports that you upload to the chat. That keeps the build the same every week, whatever the connector returns that day.

If your firm prepares the client's tax return. Check how your engagement letter and consent setup handles using client data in AI tools. Under IRC Section 7216 and its regulations, a return preparer generally needs the client's written consent before using or disclosing tax return information for purposes other than preparing the return (26 CFR 301.7216-3). The forecast runs on the client's books, not the return, but the same people often hold both.

02The Front Sheet

The Front Sheet: Five Questions Before Anyone Relies on Week Six

QuickBooks knows what's been invoiced, billed and paid. It doesn't know about the order the client agreed on the phone last Tuesday, the forklift they just financed, or the pass-through entity tax payment they've decided to make before December 31.

That matters more the further out you go. If a client's customers take about seven weeks to pay, every invoice sitting in QuickBooks today has been collected by week seven or so. From there on, the forecast runs on sales nobody's invoiced yet and payments nobody's told you about. So the workbook opens with five questions, and the answers sit on the front sheet before anyone relies on week six.

The five questions

#QuestionWhy it's there
1Have you agreed any orders or work you haven't invoiced yet, or are you expecting any big orders in the next three months?QuickBooks only sees invoices. A run rate built from last year misses the one-off order worth half a month's sales
2Do you have any loans, equipment financing, credit cards or money from owners or family that aren't in QuickBooks, or any new borrowing coming?A finance agreement signed last week has no bill in QuickBooks yet, and the payments still leave the bank
3Is anything large going out that isn't a normal monthly bill: equipment, a deposit, an annual renewal, a refund?Annual payments don't show up in a three-month average of overheads
4Is there any customer you know will pay later than usual, or earlier?Payment history can't see a dispute, a customer in trouble, or a promise made on the phone
5Are you planning to take distributions or pay any taxes from the business account in the next three months, including a pass-through entity tax payment or your own estimated tax? How much, and when?Owner payments can be the largest items in the 13 weeks, and none of them are in QuickBooks until they're paid

The 13-week cash flow tab

Weeks run across the columns, Monday to Sunday. Taxes get their own block, so the client sees tax as tax rather than buried in "money out". The line of credit works the way a US small business line usually does: closing cash below zero means the line is drawn, and headroom is closing cash plus the line limit, which is the same as cash plus the undrawn line. A short week is a week where the client would need more than the whole line.

04Time Receipts by Who Actually Pays

Time Receipts by Who Actually Pays

Most cash forecasts time receipts by the terms printed on the invoice. Customers don't pay by the terms printed on the invoice. They pay the way they've always paid, and QuickBooks already holds the record of exactly how that is.

Take a client on $1.5M in revenue whose customers pay at 50 days against net 30 terms. $1.5M ÷ 365 × 20 is $82,192 of sales landing twenty days later than a terms-based forecast says. If the client charged sales tax, the cash would be higher again, but this one sells for resale and charges none. That's $82,192 the forecast puts in the bank that isn't there, every single week, and it can be enough to hide a line of credit running out completely (section 6 shows one).

The timing rules

SituationHow the receipt is timed
The customer has at least 3 paid invoices in the last 12 monthsInvoice date plus the customer's average days to pay, weighted by invoice value
The customer has fewer than 3 paid invoicesDue date plus the delay the client gave in the five answers
Fewer than 3 paid invoices, and no estimate from the clientDue date plus the average days late of all customers. Claude flags the customer
The expected date is already pastWeek 2, and the invoice goes on the chase list
The invoice is in dispute, or more than 30 days older than the customer's averageNot forecast. Claude lists it for you and the client to decide
Sales not yet invoicedSplit by each customer's share of the last 12 months' sales, then timed by that customer's average days

The average is weighted by value so one small invoice paid late doesn't drag a big customer's timing about. The minimum of three is there because two invoices tell you what happened twice, not what a customer usually does.

Prompt · Payment history table
Using the attached QuickBooks Online exports, build a payment history
table for [CLIENT CODE]. Do every calculation in code. Use the
QuickBooks connector, for reading only, to confirm the company name is
[COMPANY NAME IN QUICKBOOKS].

1. Take every invoice paid in the 12 months to [MM/DD/YYYY].
2. For each invoice, work out the days from invoice date to the date
   it was paid.
3. For each customer, work out:
   - the number of paid invoices
   - the average days to pay, weighted by invoice value
   - the average days after the due date
   - the shortest and the longest days to pay

Weekends and holidays. A federal deadline that falls on a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday moves to the next business day (IRC Section 7503). With weeks running Monday to Sunday, that can push a payment into the next week. That's why rule T6 asks how the client pays: put the payment where the money actually leaves, not where the law says it's still on time.

Getting the money there in time. The IRS's Publication 15 for 2026 says EFTPS accepts same-day payments of $1 million or less if they're submitted before 3:00 p.m. Eastern time on a business day, and payments over $1 million must be submitted by 8:00 p.m. Eastern time the day before the due date. A payroll provider that deposits for the client may have its own cutoffs, and many take the tax from the client's account with the payroll itself, before the IRS due date. If so, the provider's debit date is the date that goes in the forecast.

Holidays in this forecast window. Veterans Day, Wednesday 11/11/2026. Thanksgiving, Thursday 11/26/2026. Christmas, Friday 12/25/2026. New Year's Day, Friday 01/01/2027. Banks follow the Federal Reserve schedule.

State taxes are inputs. Withholding, unemployment, sales tax and PTET all run on each state's own rules and dates, so they go into rules T2 to T4 as inputs you confirm. They're the figures in the forecast that Claude takes on trust, which is why the person who signs agrees them (section 10).

Never put payroll tax on the hold-back list

Withheld income tax and the employee share of Social Security and Medicare are trust fund taxes. If they aren't deposited, the IRS can assess the Trust Fund Recovery Penalty, 100% of the unpaid trust fund tax, personally on anyone responsible for paying them over who acted willfully, and Publication 15 lists an accountant among the people who can be a responsible person (IRC Section 6672). So in section 7, payroll tax deposits stay off every list of payments the client could move.

The tax rows are where strategy shows up

Look at the Taxes tab again. Most of it is compliance: the deposits and state payments are due when they're due. But four kinds of rows in a forecast like this are decisions with a tax answer behind them:

  • The PTET payment before December 31. A cash-basis S corporation or partnership deducts the payment in the year it's paid (IRS Notice 2020-75), which is why so many owners pay by December 31. Whether to elect, how much to pay and which day it leaves are tax decisions, and the forecast shows whether there's cash on that day.
  • The owner's January 15 estimate. Paid from the business as a distribution, it's a real outflow in week 13. The amount depends on the owner's whole-year picture, not just the business.
  • A year-end bonus or a year-end distribution. One is wages with payroll tax, the other isn't, and they land in different rows and different weeks.
  • A retirement plan contribution. A larger employer contribution or a different kind of plan can move the owner's tax bill by more than any single row on this page, and it has a funding date the forecast can test.

The forecast is the first place a firm sees whether the client can fund those decisions, or has cash sitting in the bank that should be doing a job. That's the point to bring in a strategist.

The three moves

They're in the order they usually land. A chased invoice can arrive in days. A supplier can agree a new date on one phone call. A bank takes longest, so ask early even if you hope not to need it.

MoveThe rule
1. Who to chaseTake the invoices due on or before the last day of the short week that the forecast lands after it, plus anything already more than 60 days overdue. Group them by customer. Score each customer: amount × average days late. Chase the highest scores first
2. What to hold backTake the supplier payments in the short week and the week before, largest first. Mark each one as a key supplier, a supplier who could take a later date, or unsure. The client decides which suppliers to ask
3. What to ask the bank forTake the lowest closing cash, add a buffer, and round up to the next $5,000. That's the temporary line to ask for. Give the weeks it's needed, and the week the client comes back inside the current line

Net pay, retirement plan deductions, federal payroll tax deposits and state withholding stay off the hold-back list (section 5). So does any tax with a fixed due date.

Distributions, the PTET payment and the owner's estimates are the owner's decisions. The prompt lists them separately, so they're in front of the owner without being part of the recommendation.

What a bank will want to see

The one-page summary covers what a lender will ask: how much, from when, until when, and why. The why matters most. A one-off pinch with a dated way out (a holiday order paid in week 11) is a different conversation from a business that runs short every month. If a distribution falls in the short week, expect the bank to ask about it.

If the pinch is an IRS payment

The IRS's route is a payment plan. On irs.gov it describes short-term plans, for balances paid in 180 days or less, and long-term plans, also called installment agreements, paid monthly. Penalties and interest keep running until the balance is paid, so paying sooner costs less. Check the current terms at irs.gov/payments/payment-plans-installment-agreements before you discuss one. A payment plan is for a tax already owed. It is never a reason to skip a payroll deposit, for the reason in section 5. Either way, the 13-week forecast is the evidence of money in and money out that conversation needs.

Set the threshold per client. For a business with weekly money in around $25,000, $5,000 is a fifth of a week. For MRW01, the firm uses $3,000, about a tenth of a normal week. For a bigger client, use a percentage of the week's forecast money in instead.
